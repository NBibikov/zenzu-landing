Cloze deletion cards — flashcards where a word is blanked out of a sentence — work well for grammar patterns, collocations, and verb forms, but work poorly for abstract vocabulary and words with several plausible answers. The difference isn't the format itself; it's whether the surrounding sentence actually constrains the answer to one word.

## What a Cloze Card Really Tests

A cloze card doesn't test "do you know this word." It tests "can you predict this word from context." That's a different skill, and it only works when the context is strong enough to do the predicting for you. This is the same logic behind the classic cloze test developed by Wilson Taylor in 1953 to measure reading comprehension: readers fill gaps using grammar and meaning cues from nearby words. When those cues are present, the task is diagnostic. When they're absent, it becomes a guessing game — and guessing games don't build durable memory.

## When Cloze Cards Work Well

### Grammar patterns and conjugation
German case endings, French subjunctive triggers, Spanish preterite vs. imperfect — these all have reliable structural cues.

*Ich habe das Buch ___ (mein) Bruder gegeben.* → "meinem." The dative-triggering verb *geben* plus the preposition-free indirect object makes the answer unambiguous.

### Fixed collocations
*Il a pris une décision ___ (rapide).* → "rapide" fits, but so could "importante" — a weak example. Compare: *Elle m'a posé un lapin ___ (hier).* → "hier," because the idiom *poser un lapin* (to stand someone up) plus the time clause anchors the slot.

### High-frequency function words
Prepositions, conjunctions, and discourse markers are ideal cloze targets because native speakers overwhelmingly choose one option in a given slot: *Trabajo ___ (desde) las nueve hasta las cinco.*

### Verb-preposition pairs
*Das hängt ___ (von) der Situation ab.* The separable verb *abhängen von* only takes one preposition — the blank has exactly one right answer.

## When Cloze Cards Backfire

### Abstract or low-frequency vocabulary
*La situation est devenue ___ (critique).* Could also be "difficile," "grave," "compliquée." A learner who answers "grave" isn't wrong about French — they're wrong about *your specific card*. This trains frustration, not vocabulary.

### Multiple correct answers
If a native speaker could naturally substitute two or three words, the card punishes correct knowledge. This is the single most common cloze-deletion failure mode reported in vocabulary-acquisition research: Paul Nation's work on vocabulary learning (Nation, 2001) emphasizes that effective practice requires unambiguous retrieval, otherwise learners stop trusting the drill.

### Sentences that are too short or too long
A six-word sentence often lacks enough surrounding context to constrain the blank. A forty-word sentence overloads working memory before the learner even reaches the gap — this is a textbook case of cognitive overload interfering with the "desirable difficulty" Robert Bjork described in his research on effective learning (Bjork, 1994): difficulty helps only when it's the *right kind*.

### Words you haven't met in context yet
Cloze deletion assumes prior exposure. Blanking out a word you've never actually encountered turns the card into a cold guess rather than a retrieval exercise.

## How to Write a Cloze Card That Actually Works

1. **Pick a sentence, not a word.** Start from real input — a line from a video, article, or podcast — not an invented example.
2. **Delete only words the sentence structurally forces.** Verbs with fixed prepositions, case-marked articles, fixed idioms, time-bound conjugations.
3. **Read the sentence without the blank.** If you can think of two natural substitutes, change the card to test recognition instead, or delete a different word.
4. **Keep sentence length between roughly 8 and 18 words.** Long enough for context, short enough to hold in working memory.
5. **Reserve pure vocabulary for word-meaning cards**, not fill-in-the-blank — test the word's meaning or translation directly instead of guessing it from a sentence.

## FAQ

**Are cloze cards better than translation flashcards?**
Neither is universally better — cloze cards suit grammar and collocation; translation or definition cards suit standalone vocabulary meaning.

**Should beginners use cloze deletion?**
Sparingly. Beginners lack the grammatical intuition to narrow down answers, so error rates stay high regardless of effort.

**Can I fix a bad cloze card instead of deleting it?**
Yes — often widening the sentence or adding one more contextual clause turns an ambiguous blank into a constrained one.
