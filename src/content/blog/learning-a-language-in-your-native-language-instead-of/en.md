## The Direct Answer

If your native language isn't English, you should learn your target language through your native language whenever possible — not through English. Routing new vocabulary through a third language adds an extra translation step, increases cognitive load, and slows down the automatic retrieval you need for fluency. English only becomes genuinely useful as a bridge in narrow cases: when it shares more cognates with your target language than your native language does, or when your native language simply lacks good learning materials.

## Why the English Detour Costs You

Picture a Ukrainian speaker learning Spanish through an English-language app. To understand the word *casa*, their brain does this: Spanish → English ("house") → Ukrainian ("дім"). That's two translation hops instead of one, and each hop is a chance for slippage, delay, or a wrong guess based on English grammar instead of Ukrainian grammar.

This matters because of **cognitive load theory** (Sweller, 1988): working memory can only juggle a handful of new items at once. Every unnecessary processing step — like an irrelevant middleman language — eats into the capacity you need for actually encoding the new word.

It also runs against the **Linguistic Interdependence Hypothesis** (Cummins, 1981), which shows that skills transfer most efficiently between a learner's strongest language and the new one. Your native language already has deep, automatic grammatical instincts; letting those instincts support the new language is faster than building a parallel scaffold out of a language you're still mastering yourself.

Research on third-language acquisition backs this up directly. Cenoz (2001) and De Angelis (2007) found that learners studying an L3 frequently pull vocabulary and grammar patterns from whichever language feels psychotypologically closer or more recently activated — and when that's a shaky L2 like English, errors and confusion increase. You end up debugging two foreign languages at once instead of one.

## When English Actually Helps

There's a real exception: cognate density. A Polish speaker learning German may find more shared vocabulary roots by occasionally cross-checking English (*Wasser/water*, *Buch/book*) than by relying on Polish alone, because English and German are both Germanic. In that specific case, English isn't a detour — it's a genuine shortcut. The rule isn't "never use English," it's "use whichever language actually reduces the number of translation hops to meaning."

## How to Study Directly in Your Native Language

1. **Set every interface to your native language first.** Dictionary mode, grammar notes, app settings — remove English as the default lens.
2. **Translate target-language sentences straight into your native language**, never through English as a stopover, even if an English translation is easier to find.
3. **Seek native-language grammar explanations.** A Portuguese explanation of Spanish subjunctive mood will map onto your existing grammatical intuitions far better than an English one.
4. **Compare cognates only when your native language and the target language are related.** Spanish and Portuguese speakers learning French, for example, can lean on real overlap.
5. **Reserve English strictly for cognate-rich pairs**, and name the exception explicitly to yourself so it doesn't become a habit for every new word.

## Worked Example

Target: German word *Verantwortung* (responsibility).

- **Via English (indirect):** Verantwortung → "responsibility" → your native word. Two semantic jumps, two chances to misfire.
- **Via native language (direct):** Verantwortung → your native word for responsibility. One jump, and you can immediately check it against the native-language example sentence it appeared in.

Over hundreds of words, that single saved step compounds into faster recall and fewer stuck moments mid-conversation.

## FAQ

**Does this mean English materials are useless?**
No — audio, native speaker content, and grammar depth in English can still be valuable raw material. The issue is the *translation path*, not the source.

**What if there are no good resources in my native language?**
Use English content for input, but do your own translating and note-taking directly into your native language instead of accepting English as the final gloss.

**Is this true for every language pair?**
Mostly, except when English is more closely related to your target language than your native language is — then cognate-checking through English is a legitimate shortcut.
