Most learners reach B2 with roughly **3,500 to 5,000 words**, not the 10,000+ figure that circulates online. That range covers everyday conversation, news articles, and most workplace communication in English, German, French, or Spanish. The exact number depends on the language and how you count "a word," but the research converges on this band far more than people assume.

## Where the numbers actually come from

The most-cited source is Paul Nation's 2006 paper "How Large a Vocabulary Is Needed For Reading and Listening?" Nation found that learners need about **98% coverage** of a text's words to comprehend it without constant dictionary lookups — that's roughly 1 unknown word per 50. To hit 98% coverage of general written text, his corpus analysis pointed to 8,000–9,000 word families; for spoken language, the bar drops to 6,000–7,000, because speech repeats a smaller, more frequent set of words.

That 8,000-word figure is often misquoted as "the B2 target." It isn't — it's closer to C1/C2 reading fluency. Nation's own CEFR mapping (Victoria University of Wellington) places B2 at around **4,000 word families**: 2,000–3,000 high-frequency words plus 1,000–2,000 words tied to the learner's own topics (work, hobbies, daily life).

Other studies land lower. Milton and Alexiou (2009), testing Greek as a foreign language, put B2 at roughly 3,250–3,750 words. Van Zeeland and Schmitt (2012) found that 95% coverage — enough for solid listening comprehension — needs only 2,000–3,000 word families. So depending on whether your goal is "understand a podcast" or "read a newspaper unaided," your real B2 target sits somewhere between 3,000 and 5,000 words.

## Why "word families" confuses the count

Nation counts in **word families**: *run*, *runs*, *running*, *ran*, and *runner* count as one family, not five. If you count individual forms instead, a 4,000-family vocabulary can mean 15,000–20,000 surface forms. This is why vocabulary-size claims online swing so wildly — always check whether a number means families or forms before comparing it to your own study log.

## A realistic path to 4,000 words

You don't need to memorize a frequency list cover to cover. Three facts make the climb manageable:

1. **The first 1,000 words cover ~75-80% of everyday text.** The next 1,000 add only another 5-6%. Returns shrink fast, which is why frequency order matters more than raw count.
2. **Learning 10 new words a day** gets you to 3,650 in a year — comfortably inside the B2 range, assuming you also review old words so they don't decay.
3. **Spaced repetition is what makes the number stick.** Vocabulary studies (Nation, 2001) consistently show that words need 5-7 spaced exposures before they move to long-term memory. A flashcard you see once and never again barely counts toward your real vocabulary.

### A simple weekly routine

- **Daily:** 10-15 new words from content you're already consuming (not an isolated list) + review of due cards.
- **Weekly:** One longer text or video, mined for 20-30 unfamiliar words in context.
- **Monthly:** A quick self-check — can you recognize 90%+ of words in a B2-level article on a topic you haven't studied yet? If not, that gap tells you where to focus next.

## FAQ

**Is 2,000 words enough for B2?**
No — 2,000 words gets you closer to A2/B1 reading coverage. B2 generally needs 3,500+ word families for comfortable, independent comprehension.

**Does vocabulary size alone guarantee B2 level?**
No. CEFR levels also require grammar control, fluency, and production skills. Vocabulary is necessary but not sufficient — you need the words usable in real sentences, not just recognizable on a list.

**Why do different sources give different numbers?**
They measure different things: word families vs. word forms, reading vs. listening, and different target languages. Always check the methodology before comparing figures.
