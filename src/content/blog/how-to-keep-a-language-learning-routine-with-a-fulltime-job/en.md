## Answer: Shrink the Routine, Don't Skip It

You can keep a language learning routine with a full-time job by compressing practice into three small, fixed slots — morning, commute/lunch, and a 5-minute evening review — totaling just 20–30 minutes a day instead of relying on one long session you'll never find time for. Research on habit formation by Phillippa Lally and colleagues (2010, *European Journal of Social Psychology*) found it takes an average of 66 days for a behavior to become automatic, but only if it's repeated at the *same trigger point* daily. The goal isn't more hours; it's reliability.

## Why Long Study Sessions Fail Working Adults

Most people quit language apps not because the content is bad, but because they planned for a 45-minute session that requires willpower they don't have after a 9-hour workday. When that session gets skipped twice, the habit dies. The fix is to design a routine around your existing schedule's dead time, not around an idealized "study hour" that competes with dinner, meetings, and exhaustion.

## The Three-Slot Routine

### 1. Morning Anchor (10 minutes)
Attach practice to something you already do every day — coffee, brushing teeth, waiting for the kettle. Review 10–15 flashcards from yesterday before checking email. This is your highest-willpower window; use it for active recall, not passive listening.

### 2. Commute or Lunch Break (10–15 minutes)
This is listening time. Play a podcast segment, a YouTube clip, or a short dialogue in your target language. Don't aim to understand everything — aim for repeated exposure. Comprehensible input research by Stephen Krashen (1982) suggests exposure slightly above your current level, repeated often, builds competence even without conscious study.

### 3. Evening Review (5 minutes)
Right before bed, do one quick flashcard pass. This is the slot most people skip, but it's the most valuable: reviewing material shortly before sleep aligns with findings on sleep-dependent memory consolidation (Jan Born et al., 2006, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*), which show that information reviewed before sleep is retained better than information reviewed at other times.

## Fight the Forgetting Curve, Not Your Calendar

Hermann Ebbinghaus's 1885 forgetting curve research showed we lose up to 70% of new information within 24 hours without review. A full-time job doesn't give you more hours, but spaced repetition gives you more *efficient* ones. Instead of re-reading a vocabulary list five times in one sitting (which research by Nicholas Cepeda et al., 2006, *Psychological Bulletin* shows is far less effective), review 15 words today, the same words again in 2 days, then in 7, then in 20. Each review takes seconds longer but saves you from relearning from scratch.

## Batch Your Content on Sundays

Spend 20 minutes once a week finding material: one article, one podcast episode, one video. Save it. This removes the daily decision fatigue of "what do I study today?" — a real barrier when you're mentally depleted from work.

## Protect the Routine From Bad Days

On days you have zero energy, don't skip entirely — shrink instead. A 2-minute flashcard review beats zero minutes, because consistency protects the habit loop even when intensity drops. Missing one day occasionally doesn't break a habit, according to Lally's research; missing it repeatedly does.

## Track Streaks, Not Hours

Counting "days practiced" rather than "minutes studied" removes the pressure to find a big block of time and instead rewards simply showing up. This matches the core principle behind habit-tracking research: frequency, not duration, builds automaticity.

### FAQ

**How long until this feels automatic?**
Roughly 2 months of daily repetition at a fixed trigger point, per Lally et al. (2010).

**What if I miss a day?**
Resume the next day exactly as planned — don't try to "catch up" with a longer session.

**Is 20 minutes a day really enough?**
Yes, if it's spaced and includes review, not just new material — consistency outperforms occasional marathon sessions.
