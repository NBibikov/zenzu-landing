## The Short Answer

A good flashcard from a heard sentence keeps the **original audio**, trims the sentence to the smallest chunk that still makes sense, and tests **one new piece of language** at a time — not the whole sentence. If you isolate one gap and attach real audio and a real-life context, you turn a fleeting sound into a retrievable memory.

## Why "Sentence Mining" Beats Word Lists

Linguist Paul Nation's research on vocabulary acquisition (Nation, 2001) found that words learned with their natural collocations stick far better than words learned in isolation. Richard Schmidt's noticing hypothesis (1990) adds the missing piece: you only acquire language you consciously notice. A flashcard built straight from something you *heard* — a podcast line, a YouTube clip, a line from a show — captures that noticing moment while it's still fresh, with the rhythm, stress, and real pronunciation intact.

A word list gives you "ahnung = idea." A sentence card gives you "Ich habe keine Ahnung, wovon du sprichst" — the actual way a German speaker expresses confusion, with the exact intonation in your ears.

## The 5-Step Method

### 1. Capture the exact sentence, not a paraphrase
Pause the audio the second you hear something new. Write (or clip) the sentence exactly as spoken, including filler words like "well," "bueno," or "alors." Authentic speech is your target, not a textbook version of it.

### 2. Isolate one unknown chunk
Pick a single word, collocation, or grammar pattern per card. If a sentence has three new things, that's three future cards, reviewed on different days. Cramming them into one card overloads working memory and weakens recall (Sweller's cognitive load theory, 1988).

### 3. Trim to under 12 words
Long sentences are hard to hold in mind during review. If the original clip runs 20+ words, cut it down to the clause containing your target chunk, keeping enough context to disambiguate meaning.

### 4. Keep the real audio, skip robotic TTS
Hearing the actual speaker's voice preserves prosody — the stress pattern that often carries meaning in languages like French or English. Review with audio-first, text-second when you can; it trains listening comprehension, not just reading.

### 5. Write a cloze, not a translation
Instead of "front: sentence / back: English translation," blank out the target piece: "Ich habe keine Ahnung, ___ du sprichst" → *wovon*. This forces production, which research on the "generation effect" (Slamecka & Graf, 1978) shows produces stronger memory traces than passive recognition.

## Worked Example (Spanish)

You hear in a podcast: *"No tengo ni idea de lo que me estás hablando, pero bueno, sigamos."*

- Trim: "No tengo ni idea de lo que me estás hablando."
- Target: *ni idea* (not "a clue")
- Card front: "No tengo ___ idea de lo que me estás hablando."
- Card back: *ni*
- Attach: the 4-second audio clip and a one-line note — "used when genuinely confused, slightly casual."

One sentence, one clean card, one testable gap.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Translating the whole sentence** on the back — this tests reading comprehension, not the language skill you actually need.
- **No audio** — you'll mispronounce the word every time you review it silently.
- **Too many blanks per card** — guessing becomes impossible, so you start memorizing card order instead of meaning.
- **Decontextualized grammar cards** ("conjugate ser in present tense") — these ignore the actual sentence you heard and lose the noticing moment.

## How Many Cards Per Day?

Hermann Ebbinghaus's forgetting-curve research (1885) is still the backbone of spaced repetition: memories decay predictably unless reviewed at increasing intervals. Modern scheduling algorithms like FSRS (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler) model this per-card rather than per-deck, which is why 10–20 new sentence cards a day is a sustainable ceiling for most learners — enough new input without drowning your review queue.

## FAQ

**Should I ever put a full English translation on the back?**
Only as a last resort for abstract words. For concrete vocabulary and set phrases, a cloze blank trains recall better than a translation.

**Can I mine sentences from TV shows or YouTube, not just audio?**
Yes — spoken dialogue in video works the same way; the visual context often helps disambiguate meaning even faster than audio alone.

**What if I can't catch the exact wording?**
Replay the clip two or three times before giving up and reaching for a transcript. The listening effort itself builds comprehension.
