The fastest way to learn phrasal verbs is to collect them from real sentences you encounter — videos, articles, conversations — and review each one attached to that original sentence, not as an isolated item on an alphabetical list. Lists teach you that "give up" means "to stop trying," but they can't teach you when native speakers actually say it, what it sounds like, or how to use it yourself. Context does all three at once.

## Why Phrasal Verb Lists Fail

A typical list — "look up," "look into," "look after," "look forward to" — asks your brain to store four unrelated definitions under one headword with no anchor. There's no sound, no situation, no emotional weight. Research on formulaic language by Siyanova-Chanturia and Martinez (2015) found that multiword expressions are processed and stored differently from single words: they're retrieved as whole chunks when learners have met them repeatedly in natural use, not when they've memorized a definition in isolation.

Webb, Newton, and Chang (2013) measured something even more concrete: learners need roughly **10 to 20 meaningful encounters** with a multiword expression before it's reliably retained — far more than for single words. A list gives you one encounter: the moment you stare at it. Context-based learning gives you a new encounter every time you meet the phrase again in something you're reading or watching.

## The Context Method, Step by Step

### 1. Harvest phrasal verbs from input, not from grammar books
Watch a video, read an article, or listen to a podcast in your target language. When you hit a phrasal verb you don't fully know — "she ended up staying," "we ran out of time" — stop and capture it immediately, with the sentence around it.

### 2. Keep the whole sentence, always
Never save "end up" alone. Save "She ended up staying for three more years." The surrounding words give you grammar (past tense, followed by a gerund), tone, and a mental scene you can replay. Nation (2013) calls this "contextualized retrieval cue" — the sentence becomes the hook that pulls the meaning back out of memory.

### 3. Group by particle, not by verb
Instead of memorizing "look up," "come up," "bring up," and "show up" as four separate puzzles, notice that "up" often signals completion or appearance: "look up" (find completely), "come up" (arise), "show up" (appear). Boers and Lindstromberg (2008) showed that teaching particles through their recurring core meanings — up = increase/completion, down = decrease, out = emergence or exhaustion — helps learners predict new phrasal verbs instead of memorizing each one from zero.

### 4. Review with the sentence, not the definition
When a flashcard resurfaces, show the full sentence first. Force yourself to recall meaning from context before checking the translation. This mirrors how you'll actually need to use the phrase: recognizing it mid-conversation, not mid-quiz.

### 5. Produce it yourself within 48 hours
Passive recognition isn't enough. Write one sentence of your own using the phrasal verb in a new context: if you learned "run out of" from a story about coffee, make your own sentence about running out of time or patience. Active production roughly doubles retention compared to recognition-only review, according to multiple studies on the "involvement load hypothesis" (Laufer & Hulstijn, 2001).

## A Worked Example

Say you hear in a podcast: *"I almost gave up on the project, but my team talked me into finishing it."* Instead of two dictionary entries, you now have one vivid scene containing **give up on** (abandon) and **talk someone into** (persuade). Group "up" with completion/surrender, and "into" with persuasion or transition — two particle families, reinforced by one memorable sentence, instead of two cold definitions.

## FAQ

**How many phrasal verbs should I learn per week?**
5–8 captured from real context is more durable than 30 from a list, because each one arrives with 10+ future review encounters already built in.

**Should I still study particle meanings explicitly?**
Yes — a short 15-minute particle-pattern lesson (up, out, down, off) once a week speeds up guessing new phrasal verbs you meet later.

**What if I forget the sentence but remember the meaning?**
That's fine and expected — the sentence is a scaffold, not the destination. Once the meaning is automatic, the specific sentence can fade.
