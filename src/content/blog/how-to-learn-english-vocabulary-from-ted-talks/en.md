You learn English vocabulary from TED Talks by watching one talk three times with different goals, harvesting 15-20 new words per talk from context rather than lists, and reviewing those words with spaced repetition over the following two weeks. Done this way, a single 10-minute talk can produce lasting vocabulary gains instead of a pleasant but forgettable 10 minutes of listening.

## Why TED Talks Are a Vocabulary Goldmine

TED Talks sit in a sweet spot most English learners never find: a single speaker, standard pronunciation, a clear argument structure, and topic-specific vocabulary repeated several times as the idea develops. That repetition matters. Paul Nation's research on vocabulary acquisition (2001) suggests a word typically needs 8-12 meaningful encounters before it sticks — and a well-structured talk naturally repeats its key terms (think "resilience," "scalable," "bias") across the intro, body, and conclusion.

They also match Stephen Krashen's "i+1" comprehensible input idea (1982): you want material that's just slightly above your current level. A TED Talk on a topic you already know something about gives you that stretch without overwhelming you.

## Step 1: Pick a Talk at Your Level

Choose talks between 6 and 18 minutes. Shorter talks move too fast to absorb; longer ones fatigue your attention before you reach pass two.

- **A2-B1 learners:** pick talks with a strong narrative (personal stories, TED-Ed animations)
- **B1-B2 learners:** pick talks on topics you already know in your own language — psychology, tech, health
- **B2-C1 learners:** pick talks with denser arguments (economics, philosophy, science)

If you understand roughly 70-80% without subtitles, the talk is at the right level.

## Step 2: The 3-Pass Method

**Pass 1 — Listen for gist (no transcript).** Watch the whole talk once. Don't pause. Your only job is to answer: what is the speaker's main claim?

**Pass 2 — Read while listening (transcript on).** Now follow the transcript line by line. Pause every time a word blocks your understanding of a sentence — not every unfamiliar word, just the ones that stop comprehension cold.

**Pass 3 — Shadow and speak.** Replay 3-4 sentences containing your target vocabulary and repeat them aloud, matching rhythm and stress. This step converts passive recognition into active, speakable vocabulary.

## Step 3: Harvest 15-20 Words, Not 50

A common mistake is writing down every unfamiliar word. This produces long lists you'll never review. Instead, cap yourself at 15-20 items per talk and apply this filter:

1. Does the word appear more than once in the talk?
2. Could you use this word in your own sentence about a different topic?
3. Is it a word a native speaker uses in everyday or professional speech (not a rare technical term)?

For each word, save the full sentence it appeared in, not just the word. "Resilience" alone is abstract; "What surprised me most was the resilience of the survivors" gives you grammar, collocation, and tone for free.

## Step 4: Space Your Reviews

Hermann Ebbinghaus documented the forgetting curve back in 1885: without review, you lose roughly 50-80% of new information within a day or two. Modern spaced-repetition algorithms exist specifically to fight this.

A practical schedule for TED-harvested words:

- **Day 0:** first review, same day as watching
- **Day 1-2:** second review
- **Day 5-7:** third review
- **Day 14-16:** fourth review

By the fourth review, most words move from "recognize" to "recall on demand." Algorithm-based systems like FSRS-5 adjust these intervals automatically based on how easily you recall each specific word, which is more efficient than a fixed schedule.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Watching with subtitles in your native language.** This bypasses vocabulary building entirely — you understand the idea but never process the English form.
- **Choosing talks purely by subject interest.** A fascinating talk at C2 level teaches you frustration, not words.
- **Reviewing words in isolation.** Flashcards with no sentence context build recognition but not usage.

## FAQ

**How many TED Talks per week is enough?** Two to three, each run through the full 3-pass method, outperforms watching ten talks passively.

**Should I take notes by hand or digitally?** Either works, but digital notes that let you replay the original audio next to the word retain pronunciation far better than text alone.
