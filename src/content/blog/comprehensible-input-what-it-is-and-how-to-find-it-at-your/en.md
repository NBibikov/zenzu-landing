Comprehensible input is language material you can mostly understand even though it contains words or structures you haven't fully learned yet. The term comes from linguist Stephen Krashen, who argued in his 1985 Input Hypothesis that we acquire language not by memorizing rules, but by understanding messages slightly above our current level — what he called "i+1" (your current level, i, plus one small stretch). Find input where you grasp roughly 90-98% and the rest clicks through context, and you're in the sweet spot where real acquisition happens.

## Why Comprehensible Input Works

Krashen's core claim is that explicit grammar study builds a "monitor" that helps you edit speech, but it doesn't build the automatic, intuitive competence native speakers have. That intuitive layer only grows from input you understand. This is why a learner who watches 500 hours of Spanish shows often sounds more natural than one who memorized 500 grammar rules but rarely listened to real speech.

Later research refined the idea. Bill VanPatten's work on input processing (1996 onward) showed learners need input that's not just comprehensible but also *noticed* — you have to pay attention to the form, not just guess meaning from context. So comprehensible input isn't passive background noise; it's active, attentive listening or reading where you're genuinely working to understand.

## The 90-98% Rule in Practice

You don't need a precise percentage calculator, but a rough gut-check works:

- **98%+ known**: too easy. You'll get fluent reading speed but learn almost no new vocabulary.
- **90-98% known**: the target zone. You can follow the main thread, guess unknown words from context, and occasionally get surprised — without feeling lost.
- **Below 80% known**: too hard. Your brain spends all its energy decoding and stops acquiring anything.

A quick test: pick a page of text or a two-minute clip. Count unknown words per 100 words. If it's 2-10 unknown words per 100, you're in range. More than 15-20 unknown words per 100 and you should drop down a level.

## Where to Find Input at Your Level

### Beginner (A1-A2)
Graded readers built for your target language are the fastest win — publishers like Olly Richards' "Short Stories" series or LingQ's graded library control vocabulary deliberately. For listening, look for "slow news" podcasts (Nachrichten leicht in German, Slow Spanish) and children's cartoons, which use simple syntax and repetition.

### Intermediate (B1-B2)
This is where authentic content becomes usable. YouTube vloggers talking about daily life, podcasts aimed at native teenagers, and news articles written in plain style all tend to land in the 90% range if you already know 2,000-3,000 words. Subtitles in the target language (not your native one) let you confirm guesses without fully translating.

### Advanced (C1+)
At this stage almost any native content — novels, interviews, documentaries — can work as comprehensible input, because your "i" is high enough that 95% comprehension is common. The skill shifts from finding easy material to noticing the 5% you don't know and converting it into active vocabulary.

## A Simple Weekly Routine

1. **Pick one piece of input daily** — a 5-15 minute video, podcast segment, or article matched to your level using the 90-98% check above.
2. **First pass**: consume it without stopping, tolerating gaps. Let your brain guess.
3. **Second pass**: replay or reread the confusing 10%, looking up only the words that block overall meaning.
4. **Log 5-10 new words or phrases** in context, not as isolated dictionary entries — "Je n'en peux plus" teaches more than "plus" alone.
5. **Review those words** over the following days using spaced repetition, since Hermann Ebbinghaus's 1885 forgetting-curve research shows unreviewed vocabulary fades within days.

## FAQ

**How much comprehensible input do I need daily?**
Krashen and later researchers like Beniko Mason (extensive reading studies, 2000s-2010s) suggest consistency matters more than volume — 20-30 minutes daily beats three hours once a week.

**Can comprehensible input alone make me fluent?**
It builds strong intuitive competence, but most learners pair it with some speaking practice and targeted vocabulary review to reach full production fluency faster.

**Is input still comprehensible if I use subtitles?**
Yes, as long as you're reading target-language subtitles, not translated ones — that keeps you processing the language itself rather than bypassing it.
